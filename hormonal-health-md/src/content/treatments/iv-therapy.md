## What is IV therapy?

IV therapy is a drip of sterile fluid, usually with electrolytes and vitamins added, given through a small catheter in a vein. Because it bypasses the gut, fluid reaches your bloodstream right away. It is a medical procedure, so a licensed professional screens you first and stays with you while it runs.

People book it when they are dehydrated after illness, travel, heat or a long night, or when they have a deficiency that is better corrected by vein than by mouth.

## Does IV therapy actually work?

It depends what you want it to do. IV fluids reliably correct dehydration, and IV vitamins reliably correct a deficiency when you cannot absorb enough by mouth. For healthy people looking for more energy or immunity, the research is thin, and the few controlled studies have not shown a clear benefit over placebo.

We would rather tell you that up front. Vitamin C and B12, for example, are absorbed well from food and tablets in most people, according to the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/). If you feel run down most of the time, a [blood test](/lab-testing) will tell you more than a drip. Start with [always tired](/conditions/always-tired).

## Is it safe, and who should not get an IV?

For most healthy adults an IV is low risk when a trained professional places it with sterile technique. The common problems are minor: a bruise, a sore vein or fluid leaking into the tissue around the site. Infection, vein inflammation and allergic reactions are uncommon but possible.

Extra fluid can be harmful for some people. Tell us if you have:

- Heart failure or another heart condition
- Kidney disease
- High blood pressure that is not controlled
- A pregnancy, or you are breastfeeding
- G6PD deficiency, which matters for high-dose vitamin C
- Any allergy to vitamins, preservatives or adhesives

## What happens during a visit?

A visit takes about an hour from start to finish. You are screened first, a small catheter is placed in your arm, the drip runs for 30 to 60 minutes while you are monitored, and you leave with aftercare instructions. The usual sequence:

1. A health screening: your history, medications, allergies and why you are booking.
2. Vital signs, including blood pressure and heart rate.
3. A small catheter is placed in a vein in your arm or hand.
4. The drip runs for 30 to 60 minutes while you sit back. You are monitored the whole time.
5. The catheter comes out, a dressing goes on and you get aftercare instructions.

If something in your screening suggests an IV is not right for you today, we will say so and tell you what to do instead.
