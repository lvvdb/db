## What is medical weight loss?

Medical weight loss is weight management run by a clinician. It starts with your history and lab work to find what is making weight hard to lose, then builds a plan from nutrition, movement, sleep and, where appropriate, prescription medication. You are monitored along the way so the plan changes when your body does.

It is different from a diet program because it treats excess weight as a health condition with causes that can be measured and treated.

## Who qualifies for weight loss medication?

Prescription weight loss medication is approved for adults with a body mass index of 30 or more, or 27 or more with at least one weight-related condition such as high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol or sleep apnea. Those are the FDA-labelled criteria, and we use them.

If you do not meet them, you can still work with us on nutrition, activity and any hormone or metabolic issue your labs turn up. Medication is one tool, and it is not the right one for everyone.

## What labs do you check first?

We check the things that commonly work against weight loss or change which treatment is safe. A typical starting panel covers blood sugar and A1c, cholesterol, thyroid function, liver enzymes and kidney function. Your medications matter too, because several common prescriptions cause weight gain.

When symptoms point that way, we add hormone tests: testosterone in men, and a broader workup in women with irregular cycles. Details are on the [lab testing page](/lab-testing). [[CONFIRM: panels and prices]]

## Which medications are used?

The medications with the strongest results today are the GLP-1 class, given as a weekly injection. They slow stomach emptying and act on appetite centers in the brain, so you feel full sooner and think about food less. Older oral medications such as phentermine are approved for short-term use and suit a narrower group of people.

In the main trials, adults taking a weekly GLP-1 medication alongside lifestyle changes lost about 15 to 21 percent of their body weight over 68 to 72 weeks. People making lifestyle changes alone lost about 2 to 3 percent. Doses start low and step up over months to limit side effects.

Whatever you are prescribed, we will tell you exactly what it is, who makes it and whether it is an FDA-approved product. The FDA has published [safety concerns about unapproved versions](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/postmarket-drug-safety-information-patients-and-providers/fdas-concerns-unapproved-glp-1-drugs-used-weight-loss) of these drugs, including dosing errors, and you should know what you are taking.

## What are the side effects, and who should not take them?

Nausea, constipation, diarrhea and vomiting are the most common side effects of GLP-1 medications, mostly while the dose is increasing. They usually ease with time, smaller meals and slower dose steps. Less common but serious problems include gallbladder disease and inflammation of the pancreas.

These medications carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors seen in animal studies. You should not take them if you or a close relative has had medullary thyroid cancer, or if you have multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2. They are not used during pregnancy. Phentermine is a controlled substance and is not suitable for people with heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure, an overactive thyroid or glaucoma.

## Is it just medication?

No. Medication lowers appetite, and what you do with that window decides how the result holds up. Rapid weight loss takes muscle along with fat unless you protect it, so the plan includes enough protein and resistance training that fits your ability. Sleep and alcohol get attention too.

Our [nutrition and supplements](/treatments/nutrition-and-supplements) service covers the eating plan in detail.

## What happens when I stop?

Most people regain weight after stopping medication. In a follow-up of one major trial, participants had regained about two thirds of their lost weight a year after the drug was withdrawn. That is the biology of appetite returning, and it is why the plan for after matters as much as the plan for now.

Before you start, we talk through what long-term looks like for you: a maintenance dose, a slow taper, or a set period on medication with a strong nutrition and strength plan underneath.

## How fast will I lose weight?

Expect steady, gradual loss. Because doses are raised slowly, the first month or two can be modest, with most of the loss coming over the following six to twelve months before it levels off. A plateau is normal and does not mean the treatment has failed.

We track more than the scale. Waist measurement, blood pressure, blood sugar and how your clothes fit often move before the number does.
