## What is testosterone replacement therapy?

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) is prescription testosterone given to men whose bodies no longer make enough. It is used when two things are true together: you have symptoms of low testosterone, and blood tests confirm your level is consistently low. The goal is a level in the normal range and symptoms that improve.

TRT is not a general energy booster and it is not approved for normal aging. The FDA has approved testosterone for men whose low levels come from a medical cause, such as a problem with the testicles or the pituitary gland. That is why a proper workup comes before a prescription.

## How do I know if I have low testosterone?

You cannot know from symptoms alone. Low sex drive, fewer morning erections, fatigue, low mood, loss of muscle and more belly fat all overlap with poor sleep, thyroid problems, depression and other conditions. The only way to know is a morning blood test, repeated on a second day if the first result is low.

If you are not sure whether your symptoms fit, read [signs of low testosterone](/conditions/low-testosterone) or take the [assessment](/assessment). If erections are the main concern, see [sexual health](/treatments/sexual-health), because testosterone is only one of several causes.

## What do the lab tests measure?

The first test is total testosterone, drawn in the morning because levels peak early and fall through the day. The [Endocrine Society guideline](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229) recommends confirming a low result with a second morning test before making a diagnosis. Fasting samples are preferred.

Depending on your results and history, a full workup usually adds:

- **Free testosterone**, when total testosterone is borderline or a binding protein called SHBG may be skewing the result
- **LH and FSH**, the pituitary signals that show whether the cause is in the testicles or in the brain
- **Hematocrit**, your red blood cell level, because testosterone can raise it
- **PSA**, a prostate marker, for men in the age range where screening is discussed
- **Estradiol, prolactin, thyroid and metabolic markers** when symptoms point that way

## What are my options: injections, gels, pellets or cream?

All forms raise testosterone. They differ in how steady your levels stay, how often you dose and what the routine feels like. There is no single best form, only the one that fits your labs, your schedule and your preferences.

| Form | How often | Worth knowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Injections | Usually weekly or every two weeks | Low cost, flexible dosing. Levels can peak and dip between doses. |
| Gels | Daily, on the skin | Steady levels. Can transfer to a partner or child by skin contact until dry. |
| Pellets | Placed under the skin every 3 to 6 months | Nothing to remember. Needs a small in-office procedure and the dose cannot be adjusted once placed. |
| Oral capsules | Twice daily with food | Newer option. Blood pressure needs watching. |
| Compounded creams | Daily | Made by a compounding pharmacy and not FDA-approved, so strength can vary. |

## How long does TRT take to work?

Most men notice changes in stages. Interest in sex and energy often improve within three to six weeks. Mood tends to lift over the first two to three months. Changes in body fat, muscle and strength take three to four months to show and keep building for up to a year.

Those timelines come from a [review of testosterone trials](https://doi.org/10.1530/EJE-11-0221), and they are averages. If nothing has changed by your first follow-up and your level is in range, testosterone may not have been the main cause, and we look again at sleep, thyroid, mood and medications.

## What happens to estrogen on TRT?

Your body converts some testosterone into estradiol, a form of estrogen. That is normal and useful: men need estradiol for bone strength, libido and healthy body composition. Most men on TRT never need a drug to lower it.

If you develop breast tenderness or swelling, we check your estradiol level. The usual first step is adjusting the testosterone dose or schedule. Guidelines do not support routine estrogen-blocking medication for men on TRT, so we use it only when there is a clear reason.

## How will I be monitored?

Monitoring is what separates treatment from guesswork. Expect a visit and labs three to six months after starting, then at least once a year. We check that your testosterone sits in the mid-normal range, that your symptoms are improving and that nothing is moving in the wrong direction.

- **Testosterone level**, timed to the form you use
- **Hematocrit**. If it rises above 54 percent, guidelines call for pausing or lowering the dose until it comes down
- **PSA and prostate symptoms**, on the schedule that fits your age and risk
- **Blood pressure, sleep and how you feel**

## Who should not start TRT?

TRT is not right for everyone. The Endocrine Society advises against starting it if you:

- Plan to father a child in the near future
- Have breast cancer or prostate cancer
- Have a raised PSA or a prostate nodule that has not been evaluated
- Have a high red blood cell count
- Have untreated severe sleep apnea
- Have severe urinary symptoms from an enlarged prostate
- Have uncontrolled heart failure, or had a heart attack or stroke in the last six months
- Have a clotting disorder

Tell us about any of these. Some are temporary or treatable, and several have alternatives worth discussing.
