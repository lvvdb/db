## What causes erectile dysfunction?

An erection depends on blood flow, nerves, hormones and state of mind all working together, so a problem in any one can cause ED. The most common causes are narrowed blood vessels, diabetes, high blood pressure, low testosterone, medication side effects, and stress, anxiety or depression. Often more than one is involved.

That is why a pill alone is not a full answer. Finding the cause tells you which treatment will work and whether something else in your health needs attention. For a plain-language walkthrough, see [erectile dysfunction causes](/conditions/erectile-dysfunction).

## What tests will I need?

The evaluation is simple. We take a history, check your blood pressure and order a small set of blood tests. The [American Urological Association guideline](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.juro.2018.05.004) recommends a morning total testosterone level for every man with ED, and we add the tests that look for the conditions behind it.

- **Morning total testosterone**
- **Fasting glucose or A1c**, to screen for diabetes
- **Cholesterol panel**
- **Other hormones**, such as prolactin or thyroid, when your history suggests them

We also go through your medications. Some blood pressure tablets, antidepressants and other common prescriptions affect erections, and there are often alternatives. Panels are listed on the [lab testing page](/lab-testing). [[CONFIRM: panels and prices]]

## What are the treatment options?

There are several proven treatments, and the right one depends on the cause, your health and what you and your partner prefer. Guidelines support choosing together with your clinician, without having to fail one option before trying another.

| Option | How it works | Worth knowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lifestyle changes | Exercise, weight loss, less alcohol, no smoking, better sleep | Improves blood vessel health and helps every other treatment work. |
| Oral medication (PDE5 inhibitors) | Tablets that increase blood flow in response to arousal | Effective for most men. Must not be combined with nitrates. |
| Testosterone therapy | Restores a confirmed low level | Helps desire, and can improve response to tablets. Only for men with low levels. |
| Vacuum device | Draws blood in mechanically | No medication involved. Takes some practice. |
| Injection or urethral medication | Acts directly on the penis | An option when tablets do not work. Taught in the office. |
| Counseling or sex therapy | Addresses anxiety, stress and relationship factors | Useful alone or with medical treatment. |
| Surgical implant | A device placed by a urologist | For ED that has not responded to other options. Needs referral. |

You will see shockwave therapy and PRP injections advertised for ED. The AUA guideline classes shockwave as investigational and PRP as experimental, because good evidence that they work is still missing. We will tell you where the evidence stands on anything we recommend.

## Are ED tablets safe?

For most men, yes. The common side effects are headache, flushing, a stuffy nose and indigestion. The important rule is that they must never be taken with nitrate medication for chest pain, because the combination can drop blood pressure dangerously. Tell us about every medication you take.

Sex is physical exertion. If you get chest pain or become very short of breath with moderate activity, your heart should be checked before starting treatment. An erection that lasts longer than four hours is an emergency and needs immediate care.

## Will testosterone fix my ED?

Only if low testosterone is part of the cause. Testosterone mainly drives desire. If your level is confirmed low on two morning tests, replacing it can restore interest in sex and make ED tablets work better. If your level is normal, adding more does not improve erections.

Our [testosterone therapy page](/treatments/testosterone-therapy) explains how low testosterone is diagnosed, who should not take it and how it is monitored.

## What about low libido?

Low desire and ED are different problems that often show up together. In men, low desire points more toward testosterone, mood, sleep, stress or medication. In women, desire commonly changes around perimenopause and menopause, and painful sex from vaginal dryness is a frequent and very treatable cause.

For women, start with [hormone therapy for women](/treatments/hormone-therapy-for-women). For men, see [signs of low testosterone](/conditions/low-testosterone).
