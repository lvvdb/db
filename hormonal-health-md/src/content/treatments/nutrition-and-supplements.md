## What is included?

This service covers two things: a practical eating plan and supplements chosen from your lab results. We look at what you eat now, what your blood work shows and what you are trying to achieve, then give you a plan that fits your week. It works on its own or alongside hormone therapy or weight loss treatment.

## Which deficiencies do you test for?

We test for the nutrients that most often run low and that cause symptoms people notice: fatigue, low mood, hair shedding, poor recovery and brain fog. A typical panel includes:

- **Vitamin D**, measured as 25-hydroxyvitamin D
- **Vitamin B12**, sometimes with folate
- **Ferritin and iron studies**, which show iron stores
- **Blood count**, to look for anemia
- **Blood sugar and A1c**
- **Cholesterol panel**
- **Thyroid function**, because an underactive thyroid mimics a deficiency

See the [lab testing page](/lab-testing) for panels. [[CONFIRM: panels and prices]]

## Do I actually need supplements?

Maybe not. Most healthy adults who eat a varied diet get enough of most vitamins and minerals. The [U.S. Preventive Services Task Force](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970) found too little evidence that multivitamins prevent heart disease or cancer, and it recommends against taking beta carotene or vitamin E for that purpose.

Supplements make sense when there is a documented deficiency or a clear reason your needs are higher. That is the approach we take: test, replace what is low, recheck, and stop paying for what you do not need.

## Who actually runs low on vitamin D, B12 and iron?

Older adults, people on metformin or acid reducers, women with heavy periods, people who eat little animal food and people who get little sun are the most likely to run low. Knowing whether you are in one of those groups tells us what to test.

| Nutrient | More likely to be low if you | How it is corrected |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin B12 | Are over 60, take metformin or acid-reducing medication, eat little or no animal food, or have had stomach or bowel surgery | High-dose tablets for most people. Injections when absorption is the problem. |
| Iron | Have heavy periods, are pregnant, donate blood often, or have a digestive condition | Iron tablets, with the cause of the loss investigated. |
| Vitamin D | Get little sun, have darker skin, are older, or carry excess weight | A daily supplement at a dose matched to your level. |

The [Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgae290) advises against routine vitamin D testing in healthy adults with no risk factors, so we test when there is a reason to.

## Are vitamin injections better than pills?

Only when you cannot absorb the vitamin by mouth. B12 injections are the standard treatment for pernicious anemia and for people whose gut cannot absorb B12 after surgery or with certain conditions. For most other people with low B12, high-dose tablets work, according to the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/).

If your level is normal, an injection will not add energy. For fluids and vitamins by vein, see [IV therapy](/treatments/iv-therapy).

## What does a nutrition visit involve?

The first visit is mostly questions. We ask what a normal day of eating looks like, when you eat, what you drink, how you sleep and how much you move. We look at your lab results alongside that, because a food diary and a blood panel together explain far more than either one alone.

You leave with a short list of changes ranked by how much difference they will make, not a rigid meal plan you will abandon in two weeks. Follow-up visits check what stuck, repeat any labs that were off and adjust from there.

## How does nutrition fit with hormone therapy or weight loss?

It decides how well the treatment holds up. During [medical weight loss](/treatments/medical-weight-loss), enough protein and regular resistance training protect muscle while fat comes off. On testosterone or menopause hormone therapy, the same habits support strength, bone and energy.

We keep the plan realistic: meals you will actually eat, protein targets you can hit, and changes made one or two at a time.

## Can supplements interfere with my labs or medication?

Yes, and it is more common than people expect. Biotin can distort several lab tests, including thyroid and hormone results. St. John's wort changes how the body handles many prescriptions. High-dose vitamins and herbal blends can affect the liver, kidneys or blood clotting.

Bring everything you take to your first visit, including protein powders, pre-workouts and gummies. We would rather simplify your list than add to it.
