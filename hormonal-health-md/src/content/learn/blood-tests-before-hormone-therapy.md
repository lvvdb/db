The tests depend on who you are and what is being treated. Men considering testosterone therapy need two fasting morning testosterone tests, plus LH, FSH, a blood count and often a PSA. Most women over 45 starting menopause hormone therapy do not need hormone levels at all, but they do need a health check and some baseline labs.

## Which tests do men need before testosterone therapy?

Men need proof of a low level and a set of safety baselines. The [Endocrine Society guideline](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229) is specific about both.

| Test | Why it is done |
| --- | --- |
| Total testosterone, fasting, in the morning | The main diagnostic test |
| A second morning testosterone on another day | Confirms the first result |
| Free testosterone | Used when total is borderline or binding proteins are abnormal |
| LH and FSH | Show whether the cause is in the testicles or the pituitary |
| Hematocrit | Baseline, because testosterone can raise red blood cells |
| PSA | Prostate baseline, for men in the screening age range |
| Prolactin, when LH and FSH are low | Looks for a pituitary cause |

Blood sugar, cholesterol and thyroid tests are often added, because the same symptoms can come from those. Read more on our [testosterone therapy](/treatments/testosterone-therapy) page.

## Which tests do women need before menopause hormone therapy?

Fewer than most people expect. For women over 45 with typical symptoms, guidelines support starting treatment based on symptoms and cycle history. [The Menopause Society](https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002028) does not recommend routine hormone testing to make the diagnosis or to choose a dose.

What matters is whether treatment is safe for you:

- **Blood pressure**
- **Personal and family history**, especially breast cancer, blood clots, stroke and heart disease
- **Screening that is up to date**, including a mammogram
- **Cholesterol and blood sugar**, as a baseline for heart and metabolic risk
- **Thyroid function and a blood count**, to rule out look-alike conditions

How treatment is chosen from there is covered on our [hormone therapy for women](/treatments/hormone-therapy-for-women) page.

## Why are hormone levels less useful for women in midlife?

Because they will not hold still. During perimenopause, estrogen and FSH swing widely from week to week, so a single result can look perfectly normal in a woman with severe symptoms. After menopause the levels are predictably low, and a test adds nothing you did not already know.

Hormone testing does help in specific cases: women under 45, women who have had a hysterectomy or use a hormonal IUD so periods cannot be used as a guide, and anyone whose symptoms do not fit the usual pattern.

## Which other tests rule out look-alike conditions?

Several common conditions cause the same fatigue, mood change and weight gain that send people looking for hormone therapy. These tests catch most of them, in men and women:

- **TSH**, for thyroid function
- **Blood count and ferritin**, for anemia and low iron
- **A1c or fasting glucose**, for diabetes and prediabetes
- **Vitamin B12**, especially with a restricted diet or long-term metformin or acid reducers
- **Kidney and liver function**

Finding one of these does not rule out a hormone problem. People often have both.

## Can I order these tests myself?

You can buy many of these tests directly from consumer lab services, and for some people that is a convenient first look. Two cautions apply. A testosterone sample taken at the wrong time of day, or while you are ill, can mislead you. And a number without your history attached is easy to over-read.

If you already have results, bring them. A clinician can tell you which are usable, which need repeating and what is still missing.

## How should I prepare for the blood draw?

A little preparation avoids a wasted trip:

1. Book a morning appointment if testosterone is being measured.
2. Fast if you have been asked to. Drink water.
3. Ask whether to pause biotin and for how long. The [FDA has warned](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/safety-communications/update-fda-warns-biotin-may-interfere-lab-tests-fda-safety-communication) that it can interfere with lab tests.
4. If you still have periods, note the first day of your last one.
5. Reschedule if you are unwell. Short-term illness shifts hormone levels.
6. Bring a list of your medications and supplements.

## What do the results change?

They decide whether treatment is appropriate, which form is safest and what to watch. A high hematocrit or an unexplained high PSA means testosterone waits until that is sorted out. Poorly controlled blood pressure or a clotting history changes which form of estrogen is suitable. Abnormal thyroid or iron results may explain the symptoms on their own.

## How often are tests repeated?

For men on testosterone, at three to six months and then at least yearly: testosterone level, hematocrit and PSA where appropriate. For women on menopause hormone therapy, follow-up is mostly about symptoms and blood pressure, with labs repeated when there is a clinical reason.
