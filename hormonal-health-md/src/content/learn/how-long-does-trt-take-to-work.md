Most men notice the first effects of testosterone therapy within three to six weeks, usually as more interest in sex and better energy. Physical changes come later: fat, muscle and strength start shifting at three to four months and settle between six and twelve months.

## What is the timeline for each effect?

Different systems respond at different speeds. The timings below come from a [review of testosterone trials](https://doi.org/10.1530/EJE-11-0221) that tracked when each effect started and when it peaked. They are averages across many men.

| Effect | First noticed | Levels off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sex drive | About 3 weeks | Around 6 weeks |
| Quality of life and energy | 3 to 4 weeks | Several months |
| Mood | 3 to 6 weeks | 18 to 30 weeks |
| Erections | Weeks to months | Up to 6 months |
| Fat, muscle and strength | 12 to 16 weeks | 6 to 12 months |
| Red blood cell count | About 3 months | 9 to 12 months |
| Bone density | About 6 months | Continues for 3 years or more |

## What changes first?

Desire. Thinking about sex more often and feeling more interested is typically the earliest reliable sign that treatment is working, appearing at around three weeks. A general sense of having more drive and getting more done tends to arrive in the same window.

These early changes are the ones most likely to tell you whether low testosterone was really the problem.

## When will my body change?

Later than most men hope. Lean mass rises and fat mass falls gradually, with changes you can measure at three to four months and a plateau somewhere between six months and a year. Strength follows the same curve.

Testosterone does not do this alone. The men who see the biggest change are the ones who lift, eat enough protein and sleep properly while on treatment. Without those, the shift is modest.

## Why is my timeline different from someone else's?

Several things move the curve:

- **How low you started.** Men with very low levels usually notice more, sooner.
- **The form you use.** Injections raise levels within days, while gels take a couple of weeks to reach a steady state.
- **Whether the dose is right.** A level that is still below the normal range will not deliver the full effect.
- **What else is going on.** Poor sleep, heavy drinking, depression and excess weight all blunt the response.

## When do side effects show up?

On a similar clock to the benefits. Oilier skin or acne can appear in the first weeks. Red blood cell counts begin to rise at around three months and peak between nine and twelve, which is exactly when follow-up labs are scheduled to catch it.

Sperm production starts to fall within the first few months of treatment, often before a man notices anything. If you might want children, raise it before you start, because it changes the plan.

## What if I feel nothing after three months?

Then two things need checking. First, your blood level: if it is still low, the dose or the form needs adjusting. Second, the diagnosis: if your level is solidly in the normal range and nothing has changed, testosterone was probably not the main cause of your symptoms.

That second outcome is common and worth knowing. In a large [trial in older men](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1506119), testosterone improved sexual function and mood modestly and did not improve vitality or walking distance much. It is a treatment for a specific deficiency, and it does not fix fatigue that comes from somewhere else.

At that point the right move is to look again at thyroid function, sleep, mood and medication. Start with [always tired](/conditions/always-tired).

## When are follow-up labs done?

The [Endocrine Society](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229) recommends a check three to six months after starting, then at least once a year. That visit covers your testosterone level, your red blood cell count and how your symptoms have changed, with a prostate check where it applies.

The timing of the blood draw depends on the form. For injections it is usually taken midway between doses. For gels it is taken a few hours after application, once levels are steady.

## Can a higher dose speed things up?

No. Pushing the dose above the normal range does not bring benefits forward, and it raises the chance of side effects, especially a high red blood cell count. The aim is a level in the middle of the normal range, held steady. Patience does more than a bigger dose.

## How should I track progress?

Write down three or four things that bother you most before you start, and rate them. Recheck the same list at six weeks, three months and six months. It is easy to forget how you felt at the beginning, and a simple record makes your follow-up visit far more useful.
