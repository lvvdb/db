There is no official "normal for your age" number. Medical guidelines use one reference range for adult men, about 264 to 916 ng/dL for total testosterone, and most define low as under roughly 300 ng/dL on two separate morning tests in a man who also has symptoms.

## What is the normal range for testosterone?

The most widely used range comes from a [study that standardized results](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2016-2935) across four large groups of men. In healthy, non-obese men aged 19 to 39, total testosterone fell between 264 and 916 ng/dL. The Endocrine Society uses the lower end of that range as its threshold.

The American Urological Association uses a slightly different, rounder number.

| Source | Low is below | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Endocrine Society | 264 ng/dL | Lower limit of the standardized range |
| American Urological Association | 300 ng/dL | Two early-morning tests required |

In the units used outside the United States, 300 ng/dL is about 10.4 nmol/L.

## Does testosterone fall with age?

Yes, slowly. Long-term studies that followed the same men for years found total testosterone falling by roughly one to two percent a year from middle age. Free testosterone, the active portion, falls a little faster because a protein that binds testosterone rises with age.

That is an average. Plenty of men in their 70s have levels that would be normal at 30. In the [European Male Aging Study](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2007-1972), excess weight and poor health were linked to lower testosterone more strongly than age was.

## Why are there no age-specific cutoffs?

Because a lower average in older men does not mean a lower level is healthy for them. Guidelines define low testosterone by a level that causes symptoms and signs, and that threshold does not move much with age. Using a lower bar for older men would simply label fewer of them, without changing how they feel.

You will see charts online that list a "normal" number for each decade. Those describe averages in a population. They are not diagnostic cutoffs and no guideline uses them that way.

## What is the difference between total and free testosterone?

Total testosterone counts everything in your blood, most of which is bound to proteins and inactive. Free testosterone is the small unbound fraction that tissues can use. Usually total testosterone is all that is needed.

Free testosterone matters when the total is borderline, or when something is changing your binding protein, SHBG. Obesity and diabetes lower SHBG, which can make total testosterone look low when free testosterone is fine. Aging, thyroid problems and some medications raise it, which can do the opposite.

## Why does the time of day matter?

Testosterone peaks in the early morning and falls through the day, and the drop is largest in younger men. A sample drawn at 4pm can look low when a morning sample would be normal. Guidelines call for a morning draw, ideally fasting, and a repeat on another morning before any diagnosis.

Illness, poor sleep and heavy training the day before can all pull a result down for a short time.

## What if my level is low-normal and I still feel bad?

Then the number is one piece of information, not the answer. A low-normal level with clear symptoms is a reason to look further: repeat the test, measure free testosterone and check the conditions that mimic low testosterone, including thyroid problems, sleep apnea, depression and medication effects.

It is not, by itself, a reason to start treatment. Testosterone therapy in men whose levels are normal has not been shown to help and carries real risks.

## What should I do with my result?

Bring it to someone who will read it in context. A clinician needs to know when the sample was drawn, whether you were fasting or unwell, and what symptoms you have. One number without that context is easy to misread in either direction.
