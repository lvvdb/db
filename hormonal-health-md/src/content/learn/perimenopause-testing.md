No blood test can confirm perimenopause. Hormone levels rise and fall too sharply during the transition for a single sample to mean much. If you are over 45, the diagnosis is made from your symptoms and the way your cycle has changed, and testing is used to rule out other conditions that feel the same.

## Why is there no test for perimenopause?

Because perimenopause is defined by a pattern over time, not by a level on a given day. Doctors stage it by cycle changes: cycles that vary in length by a week or more mark the early transition, and gaps of 60 days or more mark the late transition. That framework comes from an international [staging workshop](https://doi.org/10.1097/gme.0b013e31824d8f40).

Underneath, estrogen is swinging. It can be higher than it was in your 30s one week and very low the next. A blood test catches one moment of that.

## Is an FSH test worth doing?

Usually not, if you are over 45. FSH is the hormone people expect to confirm menopause, and it does rise as the ovaries slow down. During perimenopause it also falls back to normal between rises, so a normal FSH proves nothing.

The UK's [NICE guideline](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23) is direct about this. It advises against using FSH to diagnose perimenopause or menopause in women over 45, and reserves it for women aged 40 to 45 with symptoms and for women under 40 when early menopause is suspected.

## What about home menopause tests?

Home kits measure FSH in urine. The [FDA notes](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/home-use-tests/menopause) that these tests can detect FSH accurately but cannot tell you definitively whether you are in menopause or perimenopause, because the level changes through your cycle. A positive test is a prompt to see a clinician, and a negative test does not clear you.

## What should be tested instead?

The conditions that imitate perimenopause. This is where blood work earns its place, because each of these is common in women in their 40s and each has its own treatment.

| Test | What it rules out | Clue it may apply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TSH | Thyroid disease | Heat or cold intolerance, palpitations, weight change |
| Blood count and ferritin | Anemia and low iron | Heavy periods, exhaustion, breathlessness |
| Pregnancy test | Pregnancy | A late or missed period |
| A1c or fasting glucose | Diabetes and prediabetes | Thirst, energy crashes, family history |
| Cholesterol panel | A baseline for heart risk | Useful before discussing treatment |
| Prolactin | A pituitary cause of missed periods | Periods stopping early, breast discharge |

Low iron deserves special mention. Heavy perimenopausal periods drain it, and low iron causes fatigue and brain fog that get blamed on hormones.

## Should I order a big hormone panel anyway?

Probably not. Large panels sold online can include dozens of markers, from saliva cortisol curves to hormone ratios. The more things you measure, the more likely one will fall outside its range by chance, and that sends people chasing problems they do not have.

Testing works best when it answers a specific question raised by your symptoms. A handful of well-chosen tests, interpreted by someone who knows your history, is worth more than a long report with no context. If a result does not change what you would do next, it was not worth the money.

## When does hormone testing actually help?

Hormone testing helps when you are under 45, when you have no periods to go by because of surgery or a hormonal IUD, or when your symptoms do not fit the usual pattern. In those situations a result changes what happens next:

- **You are under 45.** Changes this early are less expected, so testing supports the diagnosis.
- **You are under 40.** Possible premature ovarian insufficiency needs a proper workup, including repeated FSH tests weeks apart.
- **You have no periods to go by**, because of a hysterectomy, an endometrial ablation or a hormonal IUD.
- **Your symptoms do not fit**, and something else needs excluding.

## What should I track at home?

Track your period dates and flow, hot flashes, nights of poor sleep and mood changes for two or three months. That record shows the pattern that defines perimenopause, and it is often more useful than a lab result. Note:

- The first day of each period and how heavy it was
- Hot flashes and night sweats, roughly how many
- Nights of poor sleep
- Mood changes and when in your cycle they happen

Bring it to your visit. It shows the pattern that defines perimenopause far better than a single number.

## When does bleeding need to be checked?

Irregular periods are expected. Some patterns are not, and [ACOG advises](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/perimenopausal-bleeding-and-bleeding-after-menopause) seeing a clinician for very heavy bleeding, bleeding that lasts longer than usual, bleeding more often than every three weeks, or bleeding after sex or between periods.

## What happens after testing?

If something else turns up, that gets treated. If the look-alikes are ruled out and your story fits, you can talk about treatment without waiting for a lab to agree. Options include hormone therapy, hormonal contraception that steadies cycles, and non-hormonal medication.
