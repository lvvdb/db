Insurance often covers FDA-approved hormone therapy when there is a documented medical reason for it. It usually does not cover compounded hormones, pellets or services sold as wellness. The answer for you depends on three things: your plan, the diagnosis on record and the exact product prescribed.

How this clinic handles insurance: [[CONFIRM: insurance and payment policy]]

## What do insurers usually cover?

Insurers pay for treatment of a diagnosed condition with an approved product. Hormone therapy fits that when the paperwork lines up. As a rough guide:

| Usually covered | Often not covered |
| --- | --- |
| FDA-approved estradiol patches, gels and tablets | Compounded creams and troches |
| Micronized progesterone capsules | Hormone pellets |
| Vaginal estrogen | Saliva hormone testing |
| Generic injectable testosterone, with documented low levels | Treatment without a qualifying diagnosis |
| Lab tests ordered to diagnose a condition | Wellness panels with no medical reason |

Your plan's formulary, the list of drugs it pays for, has the final word.

## Does insurance cover TRT for men?

It can. Plans that cover testosterone typically want what the [medical guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229) want: symptoms, two low morning testosterone results and a recognized cause. Most require prior authorization, and many will only pay for the generic injectable form unless there is a reason you cannot use it.

Testosterone for normal aging without a documented deficiency is usually not covered. See [testosterone therapy](/treatments/testosterone-therapy) for how the diagnosis is made.

## Does insurance cover hormone therapy for menopause?

Usually, for the standard prescriptions. Generic estradiol and progesterone are among the less expensive medications at a pharmacy, and most plans include them. Brand-name versions may sit on a higher tier with a larger copay.

Our [hormone therapy for women](/treatments/hormone-therapy-for-women) page explains which forms are FDA-approved and how they compare.

## Why are compounded hormones and pellets rarely covered?

Because they are not FDA-approved products. A compounded hormone is mixed to order by a pharmacy and has not been through the testing that shows a product is safe, effective and consistent from batch to batch. A [National Academies review](https://doi.org/10.17226/25791) found too little evidence to support their routine use, and [The Menopause Society](https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002028) recommends approved products when they are available.

Insurers follow that reasoning. If you are quoted a cash price for compounded hormones, ask whether an approved equivalent exists, because it may be both covered and cheaper.

## Are lab tests and visits covered?

It depends on who orders them and where. Tests ordered to diagnose a condition are commonly covered when the clinician and the laboratory are in your plan's network. Many hormone clinics do not bill insurance, in which case you pay the clinic directly.

Some clinics will give you an itemized receipt to submit to your insurer for possible reimbursement. [[CONFIRM: whether the clinic provides itemized receipts for insurance reimbursement]]

## What about Medicare?

Medicare drug plans cover many FDA-approved hormone medications when they are medically necessary, but each Part D plan keeps its own list of covered drugs and its own rules. A product covered by one plan can be excluded by another, and prior authorization is common for testosterone.

Compounded hormones are generally not covered. If you are on Medicare, check your plan's formulary for the exact product and strength before you fill a prescription, and ask your clinician whether a covered alternative would work as well.

## What does paying out of pocket usually involve?

Paying directly is common in hormone care, and it does not have to mean paying full price for everything. The parts can be split:

- **The visit** is paid to the clinic.
- **The lab work** can often be run at a negotiated cash price, which is frequently lower than the list price billed to insurers.
- **The prescription** is filled at a pharmacy of your choice, where you can still use your insurance or a pharmacy discount program.

Asking for each of those prices separately is the simplest way to compare clinics fairly.

## Can I use an HSA or FSA?

Generally yes. Under [IRS rules](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502), doctor visits, laboratory fees and prescription medication are qualified medical expenses. That makes these accounts a practical way to pay for care at a clinic that does not take insurance. [[CONFIRM: whether HSA and FSA cards are accepted]]

## How do I check my own plan?

Call the number on your insurance card, or log in to your plan's website, and ask about the exact medication and strength you have been prescribed. Coverage is decided product by product, so general answers are not enough. Ask these five questions:

1. Is this specific medication on the formulary, and on which tier?
2. Does it need prior authorization?
3. Is there a preferred form I have to try first?
4. Are lab tests covered when ordered by this clinician?
5. Is this clinic in network, and if not, do I have out-of-network benefits?

Write down the date and the name of the person you spoke to.
