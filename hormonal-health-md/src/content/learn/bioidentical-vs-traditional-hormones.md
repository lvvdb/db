Bioidentical hormones have exactly the same molecular structure as the hormones your body makes. Traditional hormone therapy can include hormones with a slightly different structure. But the difference that matters most for safety is between FDA-approved products, many of which are bioidentical, and custom-compounded ones, which are not tested the same way.

## What does bioidentical mean?

It is a description of the molecule, nothing more. Estradiol is the main estrogen your ovaries produce, so any product containing estradiol is bioidentical. Progesterone identical to your own is called micronized progesterone.

The word says nothing about where a product is made, how it is tested or whether it is safer. It became a marketing term in the years after hormone therapy fell out of favor, and it is often used as though it meant natural or risk-free. It does not mean either.

## Which FDA-approved hormones are bioidentical?

Estradiol in patches, gels, sprays, tablets and vaginal products is bioidentical, and so is micronized progesterone in capsules. Both are FDA-approved and stocked by ordinary pharmacies, so you do not need a specialty pharmacy or a custom mix to get one.

| Bioidentical and FDA-approved | Form |
| --- | --- |
| Estradiol | Patch, gel, spray, tablet, vaginal cream, tablet or ring |
| Micronized progesterone | Capsule taken by mouth |
| Estradiol with progesterone | Combined capsule |

These have been through trials, are made to a fixed strength and come with full prescribing information. Generic versions are widely available and often inexpensive.

## What are traditional hormones?

The term usually refers to older products whose hormones differ from human ones. The best known are conjugated estrogens, a mixture originally derived from pregnant mares, and synthetic progestins such as medroxyprogesterone. These were the hormones used in the large trials of the early 2000s.

They are effective and FDA-approved. They are simply not structurally identical to what the body makes, and many clinicians now reach for estradiol and micronized progesterone first.

## Are bioidentical hormones safer?

There is no good evidence that being bioidentical makes a hormone safer by itself. What the evidence does suggest is that route and type matter. Estradiol through the skin is linked to fewer blood clots than estrogen tablets in observational studies, and micronized progesterone may carry a more favorable profile than some synthetic progestins.

Those advantages belong to specific approved products. They do not transfer to compounded versions that have never been studied in the same way. [The Menopause Society](https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002028) recommends approved hormone therapy over compounded when an approved option exists.

## What is the concern with compounded bioidentical hormones?

Compounded hormones are mixed to order by a pharmacy. They are legal, and they are not FDA-approved. A [National Academies review](https://doi.org/10.17226/25791) and an [Endocrine Society statement](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2016-1271) raised the same concerns:

- No trials showing they are safe or effective in the form sold
- Strength that can vary from batch to batch
- No requirement to carry the warnings approved products must carry
- Estrogen sometimes supplied without enough progesterone to protect the uterus
- Pellets and creams that can produce higher hormone levels than intended

None of that means every compounded product is harmful. It means nobody has checked.

## What about testosterone and DHEA in bioidentical programs?

Many bioidentical packages add testosterone or DHEA alongside estrogen and progesterone. The evidence for those is much narrower than the marketing suggests.

For testosterone, the only use supported by international consensus is low sexual desire that causes distress in women after menopause. There is no FDA-approved testosterone product for women, so doses have to be kept carefully within the normal female range. For DHEA, a vaginal form is FDA-approved to treat painful sex after menopause. Routine DHEA tablets or creams for energy or wellbeing are not recommended by the [Endocrine Society](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2015-2236).

If either is offered to you, ask what it is meant to treat and how your levels will be monitored.

## When is compounding reasonable?

When an approved product genuinely will not work for you. The usual reasons are an allergy to an ingredient in the commercial product, or a need for a dose or form that is not manufactured. Some progesterone capsules contain peanut oil, for example, which matters for anyone with a peanut allergy.

In those cases compounding is a sensible medical decision made for a specific reason, with that reason written down.

## How do I choose?

Choose by asking whether the product is FDA-approved, why a compounded version would be needed if it is not, and how your dose will be adjusted. Put these three questions to anyone offering you a prescription:

1. Is this product FDA-approved?
2. If it is compounded, what is the specific reason an approved product will not do?
3. How will the dose be adjusted, and by what measure?

A clear answer to each is a good sign. Vague answers about being natural or customized are not.
