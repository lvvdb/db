For most healthy women under 60, or within 10 years of their last period, the benefits of hormone therapy outweigh the risks. That is the position of The Menopause Society. How safe it is for you depends on your age, how long ago menopause was, your medical history, and the type, dose and route of hormone.

## Where did the fear come from?

From one large study and the way its results were reported. In 2002 the [Women's Health Initiative](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.288.3.321) stopped its trial of combined hormone therapy early after finding more breast cancer, heart disease, stroke and blood clots in the treated group. Prescriptions fell sharply almost overnight.

Two details were lost in the headlines. The average woman in the trial was 63, well past the age when most women start treatment, and the trial used one specific oral combination. Later analyses that separated women by age found that those who started closer to menopause did much better.

## What are the actual risks?

They are real, and they are small in absolute terms. In the original trial of combined estrogen and progestin, the extra events per 10,000 women per year were about 8 more breast cancers, 7 more heart events, 8 more strokes and 8 more blood clots in the lungs. There were also fewer hip fractures and fewer colorectal cancers.

For women who had had a hysterectomy and took estrogen alone, breast cancer risk did not rise.

| Risk | What the evidence shows |
| --- | --- |
| Breast cancer | Small increase with longer use of combined therapy. Not seen with estrogen alone in the main trial. |
| Blood clots | Increased with oral estrogen. Lower with patches and gels in observational studies. |
| Stroke | Small increase, mainly in older women. |
| Heart disease | Depends on timing. Risk is higher when started late, not when started near menopause. |
| Endometrial cancer | Increased by estrogen alone in women with a uterus. Prevented by adding a progestogen. |

## Does it matter when I start?

Yes, a great deal. The pattern in the data is consistent enough to have a name, the timing hypothesis. Women who start within about 10 years of menopause, or before 60, have a favorable balance of benefit and risk. Women who start later have more heart disease, stroke and clots.

That is why the [2022 position statement](https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002028) frames safety around age and time since menopause, and why first-time treatment after 60 is approached with caution.

## Does the form of hormone matter?

It appears to. Estrogen delivered through the skin by patch, gel or spray does not pass through the liver first, and in observational studies it carries a lower risk of blood clots than tablets. Micronized progesterone may have a more favorable profile than older synthetic progestins.

Low-dose vaginal estrogen is in a different category altogether. It treats dryness and urinary symptoms with very little absorbed into the bloodstream.

## Who should not take hormone therapy?

Hormone therapy is generally avoided if you have:

- A history of breast cancer or another estrogen-sensitive cancer
- Vaginal bleeding that has not been explained
- A previous blood clot, stroke or heart attack
- Active liver disease

There are effective non-hormonal treatments for hot flashes, and vaginal treatments that may still be suitable. Not being a candidate for systemic hormones does not mean living with symptoms.

## What happened to the warning label?

It changed. In November 2025 the FDA announced it would remove the statements about cardiovascular disease, breast cancer and probable dementia from the boxed warning on menopausal hormone therapy, and revised labels followed in 2026. The warning about endometrial cancer with estrogen alone in women who have a uterus remains.

The change reflects the same re-reading of the evidence described above. It does not mean hormone therapy is risk-free or right for everyone. Your own history still decides.

## How is safety managed in practice?

By matching the treatment to the person and then checking in:

1. A full history before anything is prescribed, including family history.
2. Blood pressure and up-to-date screening, including a mammogram.
3. The lowest dose that controls your symptoms.
4. A route that fits your risk profile.
5. A review at least once a year.
