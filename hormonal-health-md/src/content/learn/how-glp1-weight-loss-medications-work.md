GLP-1 medications copy a hormone your gut releases after you eat. They signal fullness to the brain and slow the rate at which your stomach empties, so you feel satisfied with less food. They are approved for adults with a body mass index of 30 or more, or 27 or more with at least one weight-related health condition.

## What is GLP-1?

GLP-1 stands for glucagon-like peptide-1. It is a hormone made in the intestine that helps regulate blood sugar and appetite. Your own GLP-1 lasts only a couple of minutes before the body breaks it down.

The medications are engineered versions that last for days, which is why most are given once a week. They were developed for type 2 diabetes, and the weight loss seen in those patients led to trials in people without diabetes.

## How do they cause weight loss?

Three effects work together:

- **Appetite.** They act on areas of the brain that control hunger and reward, so you think about food less.
- **Fullness.** They slow stomach emptying, so a meal keeps you satisfied longer.
- **Blood sugar.** They help the pancreas release insulin when glucose is high, which steadies energy.

People often describe the result as the background noise about food going quiet. Eating less becomes easier instead of a constant effort.

## Who qualifies?

The FDA-approved criteria for chronic weight management are:

- A BMI of 30 or higher, or
- A BMI of 27 or higher with at least one weight-related condition, such as high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol, sleep apnea or cardiovascular disease

They are meant to be used with a reduced-calorie diet and more physical activity. A clinician also needs to confirm there is no reason you should not take one.

## How much weight do people lose?

More than with earlier medications, with wide variation between individuals. In the [STEP 1 trial](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2032183), adults on weekly semaglutide lost an average of 14.9 percent of their body weight over 68 weeks, compared with 2.4 percent on placebo. In [SURMOUNT-1](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2206038), weekly tirzepatide produced average losses of 15 to 20.9 percent over 72 weeks, depending on dose, against 3.1 percent on placebo.

Everyone in those trials also had lifestyle support. Some people lose much more than average and some much less, and results outside trials tend to be more modest.

## What are the side effects?

Mostly digestive, and mostly while the dose is going up:

- Nausea
- Constipation or diarrhea
- Vomiting
- Reflux and bloating

Eating smaller meals, stopping when full and raising the dose slowly all help. Less common but serious problems include gallstones and inflammation of the pancreas. Severe abdominal pain needs prompt medical attention.

## Who should not take them?

These medications carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors, based on animal studies. You should not use them if you or a close family member has had medullary thyroid cancer, or if you have multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2. They are not used during pregnancy.

Tell your clinician about any history of pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, severe stomach problems or diabetic eye disease, and about every medication you take.

## Do they do anything besides weight loss?

In some groups, yes. In the [SELECT trial](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2307563), semaglutide reduced heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths by 20 percent in adults with existing heart disease and excess weight who did not have diabetes. Improvements in blood pressure, blood sugar and sleep apnea have also been reported.

## What happens when you stop?

Weight tends to come back. In a [follow-up of STEP 1](https://doi.org/10.1111/dom.14725), participants regained about two thirds of the weight they had lost within a year of stopping. Appetite returns to where it was, because the medication manages the condition without curing it.

That makes the exit plan part of the starting conversation. Some people stay on a maintenance dose. Others taper with a strong nutrition and strength routine in place.

## Are all versions the same?

No. The FDA has published [concerns about unapproved versions](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/postmarket-drug-safety-information-patients-and-providers/fdas-concerns-unapproved-glp-1-drugs-used-weight-loss) of these drugs, including products that have not been reviewed for safety or quality, and dosing errors that have led to hospital visits. Ask exactly what you are being prescribed, who makes it and whether it is an FDA-approved product.
