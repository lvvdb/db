No single form of testosterone is best for everyone. Injections are the most flexible and usually the least expensive, gels and creams give steadier day-to-day levels, and pellets are the most hands-off but cannot be adjusted once placed. The right choice depends on your labs, your routine and what you are comfortable with.

## How do the forms compare at a glance?

All of them raise testosterone into the normal range when dosed properly. They differ in how often you dose, how level your blood levels stay and what can go wrong.

| Form | How often | Levels | Main drawback |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Injection | Weekly or every two weeks | Peak after the dose, dip before the next | Needles, and more ups and downs |
| Gel | Daily | Steady | Can transfer to others by skin contact |
| Compounded cream | Daily | Variable | Not FDA-approved, strength can vary |
| Pellets | Every 3 to 6 months | Steady, then tapering | A procedure each time, dose cannot be changed |
| Oral capsule | Twice daily with food | Steady | Blood pressure needs watching |

## How do testosterone injections work?

Injectable testosterone is given into a muscle or under the skin, usually once a week or every two weeks. Most men learn to do it themselves at home. It has been used for decades, the generic versions are inexpensive, and the dose is easy to fine-tune.

The downside is the curve. Levels rise after each dose and fall before the next, and some men feel that as a lift followed by a slump. Shorter intervals with smaller doses smooth it out. Raised red blood cell counts are also more common with injections than with gels, which is one reason the [Endocrine Society](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2018-00229) recommends checking your blood count on a schedule.

## How do gels and creams work?

Gels are rubbed onto the shoulders, upper arms or thighs once a day, and testosterone is absorbed steadily through the skin. That avoids the peaks and dips of injections. For many men it is the closest thing to the body's own rhythm.

The catch is transfer. Testosterone on your skin can pass to a partner or child through contact, and the FDA requires a boxed warning about this on gel products. You need to wash your hands after applying, let the area dry, cover it with clothing and wash it before close skin contact.

Creams are a different category. They are usually made to order by a compounding pharmacy and have not been through FDA approval, so the dose you actually absorb is less predictable.

## How do testosterone pellets work?

Pellets are small cylinders of testosterone placed under the skin of the hip or buttock through a tiny incision, using local anesthetic. They release testosterone over three to six months, so there is nothing to remember day to day.

That convenience has a price. Once pellets are in, the dose cannot be lowered if your level or your blood count runs high. Each placement is a minor procedure with a small risk of infection, bruising or a pellet working its way out. For that reason pellets tend to suit men whose dose is already well established.

## Are there other options?

Yes. Oral testosterone capsules taken twice a day with food are available, and their labels carry a warning about raised blood pressure. There is also a long-acting injection given in a clinic every ten weeks or so, and a nasal gel used several times a day. These are less commonly used but can solve specific problems.

## Which form is safest?

None is risk-free, and none is clearly safer overall. Every form can raise your red blood cell count, reduce sperm production and affect the prostate, which is why the screening and monitoring are the same whichever you choose. What changes is the practical risk: transfer with gels, a fixed dose with pellets, blood pressure with oral capsules.

## How do I choose?

Start from your life, then check it against your labs. A few questions usually settle it:

- Are you comfortable with a weekly injection, or would you avoid it?
- Do you have young children or a partner at home, which makes gel transfer a real concern?
- Is cost the deciding factor?
- Has your dose been stable long enough to consider something long-acting?

Your clinician will also look at your blood count and blood pressure, since those can rule a form in or out.
