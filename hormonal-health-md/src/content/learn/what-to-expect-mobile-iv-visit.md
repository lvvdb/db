A mobile IV visit brings the drip to you. A licensed professional arrives with sterile supplies, checks your health, places a small catheter in a vein and runs fluids and vitamins for 30 to 60 minutes while keeping an eye on you. Plan for about an hour from the knock on the door to the dressing on your arm.

## What happens before anyone arrives?

The visit starts on the phone. When you book, expect questions about why you want the IV, your medical conditions, your medications and your allergies. This is the first screening, and its purpose is to catch anyone who should not have an IV at home or who needs a doctor instead.

You will also be asked for practical details: your address, building access, where to park and how many people are being treated.

## What is checked when the nurse gets there?

Before any needle comes out, the clinician reviews your health history again and takes your blood pressure, pulse and often your temperature and oxygen level. They ask how you are feeling right now.

If something does not look right, such as very high blood pressure, a fever or signs of serious illness, the visit stops there and you are told where to go. That is the system working, not a failed appointment. [[CONFIRM: who performs the pre-infusion health screening, their license, and the supervising clinician]]

## How is the IV placed?

A tourniquet goes on your upper arm, the skin is cleaned and a small needle is used to guide a thin plastic tube into a vein, usually in the forearm or hand. The needle comes straight out. Only the soft tube stays in, taped down and connected to the bag.

Finding a vein can take more than one try if you are dehydrated. Drinking some water beforehand and keeping your arm warm make it easier.

## What does it feel like?

Mostly like sitting still. You may notice a cool feeling moving up your arm as the fluid goes in. Some vitamins, particularly B vitamins, can cause a brief taste or smell. Magnesium can produce a feeling of warmth or flushing, which is why it is given slowly.

Tell the clinician right away if the site burns, swells or hurts, or if you feel lightheaded, short of breath or itchy.

## How long does the drip take?

Most drips run 30 to 60 minutes, depending on the volume and what is in the bag. Add time for the screening at the start and removing the catheter at the end. You can read, work on a laptop or watch something while it runs, as long as the arm stays fairly still.

## What are the risks?

For healthy adults the risks are low. The common ones are bruising and a sore vein, and the uncommon ones are infection, fluid leaking into the tissue and, in people with heart or kidney problems, fluid overload. In full:

- **Bruising or soreness** at the site, which is common and minor
- **Infiltration**, where fluid leaks into the surrounding tissue and causes swelling
- **Vein irritation**, which can leave the vein tender for a few days
- **Infection**, which is uncommon when sterile technique is used
- **Fluid overload**, a real danger for people with heart or kidney problems
- **Allergic reaction** to an ingredient, which is rare

## Who should not book a mobile IV?

People with heart failure, kidney disease, uncontrolled blood pressure or a pregnancy should not book a mobile IV without medical clearance. Check with your own doctor first, or choose a clinic setting, if you have:

- Heart failure or another significant heart condition
- Kidney disease
- Uncontrolled high blood pressure
- A pregnancy
- A history of reactions to IV vitamins or medications

## When is an IV the wrong call?

When you are actually sick. A wellness drip is not treatment for a medical emergency. Go to urgent care or an emergency department, or call 911, for any of these:

- You cannot keep any fluids down for many hours
- Confusion, fainting or a racing heartbeat
- Chest pain or trouble breathing
- A high fever with a stiff neck or severe headache
- Signs of [severe dehydration](https://medlineplus.gov/dehydration.html) in a child or older adult

It is also worth being realistic about what a drip does. IV fluid corrects dehydration reliably. The evidence that vitamin infusions improve energy or immunity in healthy people is limited, and regulators have acted against sellers who claimed otherwise.

## What should I do afterwards?

Keep the dressing on for an hour or so and avoid heavy lifting with that arm for the rest of the day. Drink water as normal. A small bruise is expected. Call the clinic if the site becomes red, hot, swollen or increasingly painful over the following days.

## How do I get ready?

Eat something light, drink a glass of water, wear sleeves that roll up and have your medication list to hand. A few minutes of preparation makes the IV easier to place and the visit quicker:

1. Eat something light and drink a glass of water beforehand.
2. Wear sleeves that roll up easily.
3. Choose a comfortable chair near a table, with good light.
4. Have your medication list ready.
5. Let your front desk or gate know a visitor is coming.
